\title{Cross-Biome Deforestation Detection with a Lightweight EuroSAT Transfer
Classifier: Domain Shift, Label-Free Adaptation, and a Spectral-Index Baseline}

\author{Joshua Anojulu% co-author / mentor to be added before submission
\thanks{J.~Anojulu is with the University of North Texas, Denton, TX, USA (e-mail: joshanojulu@gmail.com).}%
\thanks{Code and data: \url{https://github.com/Joshua-Anojulu/satellite-deforestation-classifier}}}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Automated deforestation monitoring needs tools that are accurate, reproducible,
and cheap to run, but the accuracy a model reports on a benchmark rarely survives
contact with new geographies. We study, end to end, how a lightweight land-cover
classifier behaves when pushed out of its training distribution and across biomes.
We fine-tune a ResNet50 (ImageNet-pretrained) on EuroSAT, reaching 98.26\% test
accuracy (macro-F1 0.982), and show a 13-band multispectral variant gives no
measurable gain (98.22\%)---RGB already saturates the benchmark. We then apply the
classifier to two-date (2016 vs 2024) Sentinel-2 imagery at \emph{seven}
deforestation frontiers across \emph{three} biomes and five countries (Amazon:
Rond\^onia, S\~ao F\'elix do Xingu; Indonesian peat/palm: Riau; Congo Basin:
Tshopo, Mai-Ndombe; dry forest: Santa Cruz, Gran Chaco), validating
Forest$\rightarrow$non-Forest change against Global Forest Watch with bootstrap
confidence intervals. Two findings drive the paper. First, benchmark accuracy does
not transfer for free: a naive application to Level-2A imagery misclassifies 72\%
of rainforest as water until radiometric \emph{moment matching} is applied, and
even after matching the classifier \emph{fails catastrophically at 3 of 7 sites}
(both Congo sites and Gran Chaco, with zero or all-wrong detections on verifiably
clean composites)---a reproducible domain-shift failure. Second, a simple
label-free adaptation---\emph{AdaBN}, recomputing BatchNorm statistics on each
target scene---eliminates every catastrophic failure (e.g. Congo/Tshopo F1
0.001$\rightarrow$0.395, precision 0.02$\rightarrow$0.70, non-overlapping bootstrap
CIs) and yields the most consistent detector across biomes (lowest variance, mean
F1 0.215$\pm$0.089), though recovery quality is site-dependent. Against a classical
NDVI-difference baseline (per-scene Otsu threshold) no method dominates: NDVI has
the highest mean F1 (0.283) but is itself site-fragile, while the adapted CNN never
fails catastrophically. We argue that cross-biome robustness, not peak in-biome F1,
is the metric that matters.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\IEEEPARstart{T}{ropical} deforestation is a leading driver of biodiversity loss
and carbon emissions, with sustained global tree-cover loss over two
decades~\cite{hansen2013}, much of it driven by commodity agriculture and pasture
expansion~\cite{curtis2018,fao2020}. Authoritative monitoring---most prominently
Global Forest Watch, built on the Hansen Global Forest Change (GFC)
product---relies on large-scale pipelines. A complementary and pedagogically
important question for students and small labs is whether a \emph{lightweight,
reproducible} convolutional model, trainable in under half an hour on a single
consumer GPU, can recover a useful deforestation signal, \emph{how far it
generalizes across biomes}, and how it compares to both an authoritative reference
and a classical spectral baseline.

Most student and tutorial treatments stop at a single favorable study area. We do
the opposite: we deploy the \emph{same} classifier across seven frontiers spanning
three biomes, look for where it breaks, diagnose why, and test a cheap fix. This
turns a ``does it work?'' demonstration into a small but rigorous robustness and
domain-adaptation study. We contribute: (1) a reproducible EuroSAT classifier
(98.26\%) with preprocessing pitfalls corrected, and a controlled test showing a
13-band multispectral variant gives \emph{no} gain; (2) a demonstration and fix of
the radiometric domain shift that silently breaks EuroSAT models on Level-2A
imagery; (3) a cross-biome evaluation with bootstrap CIs exposing a
\emph{catastrophic out-of-biome failure} we show to be genuine domain shift, not
data degradation; (4) a label-free adaptation (AdaBN) that recovers it and yields
the most consistent detector; and (5) a fair NDVI baseline showing neither method
dominates across biomes.

\section{Methods}
\subsection{Dataset}
EuroSAT~\cite{helber2019} contains 27{,}000 Sentinel-2 patches (64$\times$64\,px
at 10\,m/px) in 10 classes. We use the RGB version for the main pipeline and the
13-band ``all-bands'' version for the multispectral comparison. We split 80/10/10
(seed 42) with transforms applied \emph{per split}: training receives random
flips (valid for nadir imagery); validation/test receive none. A common tutorial
error attaches one transform before splitting, leaking augmentation into
evaluation; we avoid it with a per-split wrapper. Images are resized to
224$\times$224 and normalized with ImageNet statistics.

\subsection{Model and Training}
We load ResNet50~\cite{he2016} with ImageNet~\cite{deng2009} weights
(\texttt{weights=ResNet50\_Weights.DEFAULT}) and replace the final layer with a
10-way head. Training is two-phase: (i) backbone frozen, head trained 10 epochs
(Adam, lr $10^{-3}$); (ii) all layers fine-tuned 8 epochs (lr $10^{-4}$).
Cross-entropy loss, batch size 64, automatic mixed precision, best checkpoint by
validation accuracy. Training took $\sim$20\,min on an NVIDIA RTX 4060 (8\,GB),
CUDA 12.6, PyTorch 2.12, fixed seed. For the \emph{multispectral} variant we
replace the first convolution with a 13-channel layer initialized by weight
inflation (each channel seeded with the mean of the pretrained RGB filters, scaled
$3/13$) and normalize per band; training is otherwise identical.

\subsection{Study Areas and Imagery}
We selected seven documented frontiers across three biomes, each biome replicated
(Table~\ref{tab:sites}), confirmed on the GFW loss layer. For each we retrieved
cloud-filtered ($\leq$25\%) median composites of B04, B03, B02 (RGB) plus B08 (NIR)
Sentinel-2 Level-2A~\cite{drusch2012} for the 2016 and 2024 dry seasons
(June--September) from the Copernicus Data Space Ecosystem via openEO. The same
seasonal window each year avoids phenology-driven false change. Scenes are
$\sim$2{,}700--2{,}800\,px per side at 10\,m/px in their local UTM zones (20S--47N),
confirming the pipeline is not tied to one projection or hemisphere. Composite
diagnostics (valid-pixel fraction, nodata, saturation, mean NDVI) confirm all 14
scenes are clean (100\% valid, $\sim$0\% nodata, $<$3.5\% saturated); the Congo
scenes have healthy mean NDVI (Tshopo 0.66, Mai-Ndombe 0.58--0.61) and 58--75\%
high-vegetation cover, so their classifier failures (Sec.~\ref{sec:crossbiome}) are
domain shift, not data.

\subsection{Radiometric Moment Matching}
EuroSAT derives from hazier, less atmospherically corrected imagery with a blue
cast (global RGB mean $\approx(86,97,103)$). Modern L2A composites are
atmospherically corrected, so a naive render is catastrophically misclassified
(72\% of a rainforest scene labeled water). We correct this by per-channel moment
matching: each channel is linearly rescaled so its mean and standard deviation
match EuroSAT's global statistics, with both dates matched to the same reference.

\subsection{Change Detection and Validation}
Each scene is tiled into georeferenced 64$\times$64 (640\,m) patches on a 32\,px
stride ($\sim$2$\times$ finer grid) preserving the 640\,m footprint. A cell is
flagged as deforestation if classified Forest in 2016 and \{AnnualCrop, Pasture,
Industrial, Residential, PermanentCrop\} in 2024. We validate against the Hansen
GFC-2024-v1.12 \texttt{lossyear} raster: a reference cell is ``loss'' if a fraction
$f$ of its 30\,m pixels record loss in $(2016,2024]$; we report $f\in\{0.25,0.5\}$.
Each per-site F1 carries a 95\% bootstrap CI (1{,}000 cell resamples, seed 42);
across-site differences use a Wilcoxon signed-rank test ($n{=}7$).

\subsection{Domain Adaptation: AdaBN}
Domain shift moves intermediate activation distributions, which BatchNorm
normalizes using \emph{source} (EuroSAT) statistics. AdaBN~\cite{li2016} replaces
these with \emph{target} statistics: we reset each BatchNorm~\cite{ioffe2015}
layer to cumulative-average mode and run label-free forward passes over the target
scene before classifying, so each scene/date is normalized to its own radiometry.
This needs no labels, no retraining, and one extra forward pass.

\subsection{Classical Baseline: NDVI-Difference}
As a non-learning reference we compute the NDVI~\cite{tucker1979}
$=(\mathrm{NIR}-\mathrm{Red})/(\mathrm{NIR}+\mathrm{Red})$ per pixel for both
dates, aggregate to the same grid,
and flag a cell as loss if it was forest in 2016 (NDVI $\geq\tau$) and dropped by
$\geq$0.2 by 2024. To avoid biasing the baseline, $\tau$ is chosen \emph{per scene}
by Otsu's method~\cite{otsu1979} (clamped to $[0.35,0.70]$), adapting to biome
phenology (dry forest has lower NDVI than wet evergreen forest).

\section{Results}
\subsection{Classification and an RGB-vs-Multispectral Null Result}
The RGB classifier reaches 98.26\% test accuracy (2700 images; best val 98.52\%;
macro-F1 0.982), all per-class F1 $\geq$0.97, Forest recall 0.997
(Fig.~\ref{fig:cm}). The 13-band multispectral model reaches 98.22\% (macro-F1
0.981)---statistically indistinguishable. The ten extra bands give no gain (RGB
saturates EuroSAT), justifying the RGB-only deforestation pipeline.

\subsection{Controlled Domain-Adaptation Ablation}
Lacking labeled Sentinel-2 ground truth, we compare adaptation methods on a
controlled nonlinear radiometric shift of the labeled EuroSAT test set
(Table~\ref{tab:ablation}). An unadapted classifier loses $\sim$47 points; moment
matching (the pipeline default) recovers only $\sim$28\% under a nonlinear shift;
\emph{AdaBN recovers 99\% with no labels}, motivating its use on real scenes.

\subsection{Cross-Biome Detection: CNN vs AdaBN vs NDVI}
\label{sec:crossbiome}
We evaluate three detectors at all seven sites against GFW
(Fig.~\ref{fig:multisite}, Table~\ref{tab:crossbiome}). Four findings: (a) the
plain CNN \emph{fails catastrophically at 3 of 7 sites} (F1 $\leq$0.001 at Tshopo,
Mai-Ndombe, and Gran Chaco---zero or all-wrong detections), labeling non-European
forest as non-forest; both independent Congo sites fail, so this is a reproducible
domain-shift failure on clean composites; (b) \emph{AdaBN eliminates every
catastrophic failure}, e.g. Congo/Tshopo to F1 0.395, precision 0.699, with
non-overlapping bootstrap CIs ($[0.00,0.00]$ vs $[0.37,0.42]$); recovery is
site-dependent (full at Tshopo, noisier at Mai-Ndombe, which over-detects); (c)
AdaBN buys \emph{robustness}: it is the most consistent detector (lowest variance,
never zero) though its mean F1 (0.215) trails NDVI; (d) NDVI has the highest mean F1
(0.283) and wins 4/7 sites but is itself \emph{site-fragile}---it wins Gran Chaco
dry forest yet collapses at Santa Cruz (0.010), where the low NDVI dynamic range
defeats the absolute-drop rule. With $n{=}7$ sites no across-site difference is
significant (Wilcoxon $p>0.10$); the robust effects are per-site (non-overlapping
CIs). We therefore claim robustness and biome-dependence, not a single winner.

\section{Discussion}
The headline lesson is that in-benchmark accuracy poorly predicts cross-biome
behavior. A 98\%-accurate classifier fails two ways in the wild---silently
(radiometric shift) and catastrophically out-of-biome, at nearly half our sites and
reproducibly across two independent Congo frontiers---and single-site evaluation
hides both. The fix is cheap: AdaBN needs no labels, no retraining, and one forward
pass, yet eliminates every catastrophic failure and makes the detector the most
consistent across biomes---a favorable trade for a screening tool, where a
catastrophic blind spot is far worse than a few points of mean F1 (even though NDVI's
mean F1 is higher here). The NDVI comparison keeps the CNN honest: a classical index
has the highest mean F1, but is itself site-fragile (it collapses at one dry-forest
site), so the methods are complementary and none is safe alone. \textbf{Limitations:}
(i) $n{=}7$ sites still limits across-site statistical power (two further sites were
blocked by Copernicus availability); (ii) 640\,m patches miss small/linear clearings,
capping recall; (iii) the NDVI baseline uses an absolute-drop rule (a relative
variant might be fairer in dry forest); (iv) AdaBN adapts only BatchNorm (second
-order) statistics---deeper shift is unaddressed; (v) European training applied to
the tropics; region-matched labels would reduce the underlying shift.

\section{Conclusion}
A lightweight, reproducible ResNet50/EuroSAT pipeline recovers a deforestation
signal that agrees with Global Forest Watch---but only within its comfort zone.
Pushed across seven frontiers it fails outright at three of them (both Congo Basin
sites and a dry-forest site), a reproducible failure we attribute to genuine domain
shift and repair with a label-free AdaBN pass, yielding the most consistent detector
across three biomes while a classical NDVI baseline has the highest mean F1 yet is
itself site-fragile; no detector dominates. Robustness across biomes, not peak in-biome F1,
is the metric that matters. Future work: more sites per biome for power; a
shift-magnitude gate for selective adaptation; region-matched fine-tuning and
deeper adaptation beyond BatchNorm; finer patches for sub-patch recall; and
multi-temporal sequences beyond two snapshots.
